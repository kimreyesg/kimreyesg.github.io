## Computational materials science

<p class="section-intro">
Modern materials research combines quantum mechanics, numerical simulation, and increasingly data-driven methods. These are two of the main tools that shape my research and student projects.
</p>

<div class="feature-grid two-column-grid">
  <details class="details-card">
    <summary>Density Functional Theory</summary>
    <p>
      First-principles methods allow us to calculate electronic structure and material properties from quantum mechanics. In practical DFT calculations, the Kohn–Sham equations and the charge density are solved self-consistently until the electronic ground state converges.
    </p>
    <pre>Initial charge density ρ(r)
        ↓
Effective potential Veff(r)
        ↓
Solve Kohn–Sham equations
        ↓
Update ρ(r)
        ↓
Check convergence
   ↙           ↘
 No            Yes
Loop        Properties</pre>
  </details>

  <details class="details-card">
    <summary>Machine Learning in Materials Discovery</summary>
    <p>
      Machine-learning models can complement physics-based simulations by learning relationships between material descriptors and calculated properties, accelerating screening across large chemical and structural spaces.

## Materials science in unexpected places

<p class="section-intro">
Materials science has a long history of useful discoveries emerging from observation, experimentation, and occasionally pure accident.
</p>

<div class="feature-grid two-column-grid">
  <details class="details-card compact-details">
    <summary>Saccharin: an unexpected laboratory observation</summary>
    <p>In 1879, Constantin Fahlberg noticed a sweet taste after working with coal-tar derivatives. The observation eventually led to saccharin, the first artificial sweetener.</p>
  </details>

  <details class="details-card compact-details">
    <summary>Stainless steel: a sample that did not rust</summary>
    <p>Harry Brearley was studying steel alloys when a chromium-containing sample resisted corrosion, contributing to the development of stainless steel.</p>
  </details>

  <details class="details-card compact-details">
    <summary>Teflon: an unexpected solid</summary>
    <p>In 1938, Roy Plunkett found that a refrigerant gas had polymerized into a slippery white solid, later known as PTFE or Teflon.</p>
  </details>

  <details class="details-card compact-details">
    <summary>Vulcanized rubber: heat, sulfur, and durability</summary>
    <p>Charles Goodyear's experiments with rubber and sulfur helped produce a material that remained elastic and durable over a much wider temperature range.</p>
  </details>
</div>
